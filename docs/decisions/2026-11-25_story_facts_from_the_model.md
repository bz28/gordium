# The story facts, worked out from the model (2026-11-25)

**Purpose:** how Gordium works out the story facts the Building Code's single-exit tables read (which story is the first, second or fourth above grade plane; the dwelling units on a story; whether its rooms exit directly outside) from the model, instead of asking the architect.\
**For:** engineers changing `model_measurement/routes/story_place.py` (and the later story facts); reviewers of the rules that read them.\
**Kept current by:** the engineer who changes how a story fact is worked out, in that same branch.

Ben, 2026-11-25: everything the model can show is worked out by Gordium; only what it can't know for certain goes to the architect. Before this, the five facts had no source at all (no field fed them), so the rules reading them (BCNYS 1006.3.3, Tables 1006.3.4(1) and (2), 1007.1.1) always needed review on a real model.

## The law (2025 Building Code of New York State, quoted from the book)

- **GRADE PLANE** (p.48): "A reference plane representing the average of finished ground level adjoining the building at exterior walls. Where the finished ground level slopes away from the exterior walls, the reference plane shall be established by the lowest points within the area between the building and the lot line or, where the lot line is more than 6 feet (1829 mm) from the building, between the building and a point 6 feet (1829 mm) from the building."
- **STORY ABOVE GRADE PLANE** (p.63): "Any story having its finished floor surface entirely above grade plane, or in which the finished surface of the floor next above is: 1. More than 6 feet (1829 mm) above grade plane; 2. More than 12 feet (3658 mm) above the finished ground level at any point; or 3. More than 6 feet (1829 mm) above the finished ground level for more than 50 percent of the total building perimeter."
- **BASEMENT** (p.34): "A story that is not a story above grade plane".
- Table 1006.3.4(2) (p.317): "First story above or below grade plane", "Second story above grade plane"; Table 1006.3.4(1): "Basement, first, second or third story above grade plane", "Fourth story above grade plane and higher".

## Step 1: a story's place (this PR)

1. **The stories** are the levels with rooms, each with the range of its rooms' finished floor elevations and its level's own elevation (a sunken room widens it). A split level (two stories' floors overlapping in height) leaves every place not measured. Every level of the model must be listed (`all_levels`: the Revit reader sends every one since review round 2, one with nothing drawn on it too), else nothing is placed; a description not read whole (a room on no level read) places nothing either. A level with no rooms (not at a story's floor) may be a story nobody drew rooms on (a garage, a lobby, an unfinished basement) or no story (a roof, a foundation datum): both are tried (up to 8 such levels), so a datum under a slab changes nothing, but a garage level under the first rooms leaves their number open.
2. **The ground** is only what the architect stated, from the lot facts:
   - **flat ground** (`ground_is_flat`, `grade_elevation`): the grade plane at most that elevation, and a known point of finished ground (item 2). Finished ground stated higher at an opening contradicts it (nothing placed). Flat ground the host only *assumed* (`ground_assumed`) is not used.
   - **spot grades**, finished only (the Revit add-in's "Finished spot grades", or ground the architect states finished, Rhino's "No regrading near the house"; Revit's plain "Spot grades" are the existing ground, as Rhino's Grade layer, so they place nothing): every wall of the stories' outline, a courtyard's included, must have a spot within 2 ft outside that story's own outline (the zoning reader's own reach), else nothing is placed: the ground floor's walls under an upper story's overhang keep their spots. A spot inside a story's outline is under it, not ground beside it. Outlines that come apart (separate buildings) aren't placed. **Gordium's reading** (the usual surveying reading, said with every figure measured from spots and in the rules' notes): the ground between two spots lies between their values, so the average along the walls, and so the grade plane, is at most the highest spot. The slope-away rule can only lower the grade plane, with no lower end the spots show: a story is never proven a basement from spot grades. A spot is never item 2's "finished ground level at any point": it may stand in an areaway or a window well, whose floor as "ground" is open question 4.6 (docs/questions_for_architects.md); only flat ground gives that point.
   - **existing ground** (`grade_is: existing`), or **none**: nothing is placed.
3. **Each story** is certainly above grade plane (at the grade plane's top end, with 1/16 in to spare) or could be either: **a story is never proven a basement** (review round 6: that needs the finished ground at every point beside the building, and rounds 3 to 6 each found another way the model can hold ground below the stated ground or a higher floor next above). Below the top story, being above grade plane holds for every story above one that is (each test only gets easier higher up), so those readings are "the lowest b stories are basements"; the top story has no floor next above, so only its own floor decides it, whatever is under it (a lower story can be above grade plane by item 2 alone while the top one's floor is under the grade plane: then the top isn't placed). **A fact is measured only where every reading gives the same answer**, else it needs review and says which stories it could be. A slab at or above the stated ground is the first story above grade plane, and the stories over it are numbered; a building with a basement, or a level with no rooms under its lowest rooms (a foundation datum), leaves the numbers above open: a level mark saying "not a story" is the likely follow-up.
4. **Plumbing:** `check._story_ground` reads the lot facts into `story_place.ground_of`; `egress.check(..., ground=)` hands it to `routes.measure`.

**How we know:** `packages/model_measurement/tests/test_routes_story_place.py` walks 15 stacks of floors (basements, sunken rooms, floors on each threshold) with 5 sets of levels with no rooms (none, a garage under the rooms, a datum, a roof, a gap) over 21 grounds and none: an oracle written from the three definitions lists every world the stated ground allows (a grade plane anywhere at or below its top end, the lowest ground, the ground along half the perimeter, which height of a stepped floor counts as "the floor next above", each level with no rooms a story or not) and every fact measured must be the oracle's one value; flat ground must settle every stack whose lowest story is above it and clear of the thresholds. The real models: the four-unit apartment (as sent, its levels not all listed and no ground: not placed; with every level listed and its flat ground at 0, Level 1 first and Level 2 second; at 5 ft, Level 1 the first above or below and Level 2 not placed), and 106 Ithaca Rd's levels and four spot grades (Level 1 the first story; the Roof tried as a story and not). Through the check (`packages/design_checker/tests/test_story_place_check.py`), a one-exit Group A version of the apartment fails Table 1006.3.4(2) on flat ground at 0 (its second story), needs review at 5 ft, and needs review, never a pass, wherever the place isn't measured.

**Not done:** the Residential Code's stated `stories_above_grade_plane` isn't used as a cross-check (a different book: Ben through the coordinator, 2026-11-25). A level with rooms whose outline is at most 2/3 of the story below's (or not given) may be a mezzanine (505.2: part of the story below): tried both ways, never placed itself (review round 7).

## Steps 2 and 3 (next PRs)

2. **Rooms that exit directly outside** (1006.3.4 item 2: "Rooms, areas and spaces complying with Section 1006.2.1 with exits that discharge directly to the exterior at the level of exit discharge, are permitted to have one exit or access to a single exit", p.317). Done (`routes.rooms_exit_outside`), read as **spaces** (the item's own "rooms, areas and spaces"; Gordium's reading, shown to confirm): rooms joined by doors or passages without crossing a corridor, a lobby, an exit or the outside are one space, whose occupants reach its exit through it (1016.2: a bedroom off a living room, a workroom or closet off a shop); a corridor or lobby opening into one space only is a vestibule of it, one opening into two or more is shared (`routes/spaces.py`). Yes only where every space has its own door to the outside marked as an exterior exit door at discharge. No only where a space reaches the outside through a corridor shared with another tenant, which the model shows only for dwelling units: every room of the hall is marked a corridor (a lobby may be a dwelling's own foyer), it reaches an exit itself, it is the space's one way out of itself (no other door or opening, into an exit or another hall, and no stair within it), two or more of the spaces opening into it hold a room whose function is residential, and the building states no corridor is within a dwelling unit (`corridors_within_dwelling_units` no, a fact the Building Code check already reads for 1020.2 exception 2). From the drawing alone a corridor within one tenant's space (a shop's back corridor to its toilets, a house's hall) can't be told from one shared between tenants. Not measured otherwise (a door outside not marked so, or onto an exterior stair; a space whose way out is through a corridor that may be its own, or neither; the story's routes not read). The rules read it only in their review conditions: yes keeps a one-exit story at needs review, no lets it fail where it fails, so it never makes a pass. **How we know:** `packages/model_measurement/tests/test_routes_rooms_exit_outside.py` walks a row of three rooms over every mix of door kinds (marked, not marked, onto a stair, none) and joins (door, passage, wall), and again with a corridor each room opens into or not (shared, a vestibule, or opening into none), with and without its own exit, the rooms residential or with no function, the corridor fact stated no or not stated: every mix, against an oracle written separately from the reading above; the four-unit apartment is no on both stories once its apartments' function and the corridor fact are stated (its apartments reach the outside only through the shared corridor and the stair enclosures, which are exits); as Revit sent it, not measured.
3. **Dwelling units on a story:** groups of rooms joined by doors and passages without passing through a corridor, lobby, exit or the outside; a group with one entrance and a room whose function is residential is one unit; two or more entrances, no entrance (part of a unit on two stories), an unknown space or a group with no function stated: not measured (or low and high readings, passing only at the high count). If the not-measured case is common on real models, a room mark (Gordium: Unit) follows.

## Review round 1 (2026-11-25)

Four findings, three confirmed by both skeptics, each a way a place could be measured that the law doesn't settle: a level with no rooms under the lowest rooms was ignored (a garage level would have made the first rooms the second story); the top story was taken as above grade plane whenever a story under it was, though a lower story can be above by item 2 alone (more than 12 ft over the lowest ground) while the top story's floor is under the grade plane; a spot grade inside the outline counted as finished ground. The fourth (two buildings pooling their spot grades) was confirmed by one skeptic and refuted by the other (one outline per level can't hold two buildings; outlines of different levels apart can): its fix (outlines that come apart aren't placed) is kept as the cautious reading, with courtyard walls now needing spots too. The walk's oracle now tries every level with no rooms as a story and not, gives no place to a story under the grade plane above one that is above it, and its grounds include spots more than 12 ft apart: at `ee58c234` it fails; now it passes.

## Review round 2 (2026-11-25)

Two findings confirmed by both skeptics, each a possible false pass: the Revit reader sent only levels with a room, door or enclosed space drawn on them, so an open parking story at the ground never arrived and the first rooms above it measured the first story (through the check, a one-exit Group A story failing as drawn passed with its ground floor left empty); and spot grades were judged against the union of every story's outline, so ground at the ground floor's own walls under an overhang was dropped and the grade plane's top end came out too low (a sub-basement measured the first story below). Fixed: the reader sends `all_levels` (RouteReader.cs, pinned in RouteTests.cs), and without it nothing is placed; each story's walls take spots from outside that story's own outline. Two more taken the cautious way without skeptics: item 2's point never comes from a spot (open question 4.6), and a description not read whole places nothing. One test per finding (`test_routes_story_place.py`, round 2).

## Review round 3 (2026-11-25)

Two findings, fixed the cautious way: flat ground proved a basement even where the description states lower finished ground at an opening or a well's floor (item 2's "at any point": a possible false pass); and Revit's plain "Spot grades", the existing ground in Gordium's own convention, were taken as finished. One test each (`test_routes_story_place.py`, round 3).


## Review round 4 (2026-11-25)

Round 3's check of finished ground stated at an opening was made for flat ground only: with spot grades, ground stated higher than every spot left the highest spot as the grade plane's top end, though the grade plane could be higher (a second basement measured the first below). Now stated ground above the highest spot places nothing, as for flat ground. One test.

## Review round 5 (2026-11-25)

Three more, each a possible false pass, fixed the cautious way: one level's rooms more than 4 ft apart in height (an upper story's rooms drawn on a lower level with an offset), or a level within a story's floors, now place nothing (before, one story: the fourth story measured not the fourth); with spot grades, the grade plane's top end is the highest finished spot outside the building, not only the ones within 2 ft of the walls (under the reading, ground between a wall's spot and a higher one farther out rises toward it); and on flat ground a door to the outside from a room under the ground, or a stair's foot under it, unsettles the grade plane as an area well does (an areaway, question 4.6). One test each; the walk's oracle refuses a level within a story's floors too.

## Review round 6 (2026-11-25)

Three more ways a basement was "proven" on flat ground that the law doesn't settle: a window sill under the ground with no well given, a door to the outside from an enclosed space with no room, a double-height room whose ceiling rises past the next story's floor (the floor next above may be the one after). Rounds 3, 5 and 6 each found holes of this one class, so it is closed as a class: a basement is never proven. One test for the three cases; the tests that leaned on a proven basement now say the story isn't placed.

## Review round 7 (2026-11-25)

Two more, each a possible false pass: a spot under an upper story's overhang more than 2 ft from the ground floor's walls was dropped from the grade plane's top end (now every finished spot counts; more only ever raises it); and a mezzanine drawn on its own level, taken as a story, proved the story under it above grade plane by item 1 (now a level whose outline is at most 2/3 of the one below, BCNYS 505.2.1's most, or not given, is tried as a mezzanine too and never placed itself). The oracle's flat-ground worlds now range over every grade plane at or below the ground, as the code reads it, so a basement proven again would fail the walk. Words: "the highest finished spot grade"; the unused grade-plane low end and flat keys removed.

## Review round 1 of step 2 (2026-11-25)

The first reading took every room as needing its own door outside: a bedroom off a living room with its own exit, or a workroom off a shop, made the story "no", which removed the item 2 review and let a one-exit story fail outright (never a pass: the fact is read only in review conditions). A closet whose room had no use given was likewise a room "without an exit". Fixed as a class: the answer is per space (rooms joined by doors or passages), "no" only for a space reaching the outside through a shared corridor or lobby, every other unsettled case not measured. The walk now has a corridor and its oracle the spaces reading; one test per case found.

## Review round 2 of step 2 (2026-11-25)

A corridor touching two or more spaces was taken as shared, so a shop's back corridor to its own toilets, or a house's hall marked a corridor, read "no" and failed a one-exit story outright (never a pass). The class: from the drawing alone no corridor is known to be shared between tenants. "No" now needs the corridor shared between dwelling units the building says have no corridor within them, and the corridor reaching an exit; every other such space is not measured. The walk varies the corridor's exit, the rooms' function and the corridor fact, with an oracle written apart; one test for the shop and the house.

## Review round 3 of step 2 (2026-11-25)

Two more wrong "no"s (never a pass): a house's foyer marked a lobby counted as a shared corridor (the corridor fact says nothing of lobbies, and Gordium suggests "lobby" for a room named foyer), and a space with its own door into a stair enclosure, opening onto the shared corridor too, read "no" though that door may be its direct way out. Now only a hall marked a corridor throughout can be shared, and such a space is not measured. The walk varies the hall's use; one test for each.

## Review round 4 of step 2 (2026-11-25)

Two more wrong "no"s of round 3's kind: a living room on the shared corridor whose own foyer (with the front door) also opens into a coat closet (the foyer, touching two spaces, wasn't merged into it), and a space joined to a stair enclosure by an opening with no door. As a class: "no" stands only where the shared corridor is the space's one way out of itself; any other door or opening leaves it not measured. One test for both.

## Review round 5 of step 2 (2026-11-25)

One more wrong "no": a stair within a space (a two-story unit's own stair down to its front door) wasn't seen as another way out; now any stair the description gives whose nosings stand in the space (or can't be placed) leaves it not measured. Wording: the fact's meaning says the one-way-out condition; an opening is named by its rooms' names.
